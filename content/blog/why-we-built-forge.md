The idea for Forge started with a spreadsheet. Not a business plan — a log.

For three months I tracked every place I tried to work from in Lagos: cafés, hotel lobbies, a friend's spare office, two coworking spaces, my own dining table. For each one I noted four things: whether the power held, whether the internet held, whether I could take a call without apologising for the background noise, and how much of the day I actually spent building.

The last column was brutal. On a good day I got five productive hours. On a normal day, three.

## The problem isn't desks

There is no shortage of desks in this city. What is scarce is a room where nothing interrupts you.

The failure modes were boringly consistent:

- **Power.** Grid goes, generator takes 40 seconds to catch, your dev container dies, you lose the thread you were holding in your head. Twice a day.
- **Internet.** Advertised as "high-speed", measured at 4 Mbps down with 300 ms of jitter. Fine for email. Not fine for a video call, a large `git push`, or a CI run you actually need to watch.
- **Noise.** Somebody's meeting is everybody's meeting.
- **Furniture.** A café stool is a 45-minute chair being asked to do an eight-hour job.

Every one of these is solvable. None of them are glamorous. That is precisely why they stay unsolved — nobody puts "the generator switches over in under two seconds" on a billboard.

## What we decided to optimise for

We built Forge around one metric: **uninterrupted hours**. Not desks sold, not events hosted, not community size. Hours where a person sat down and nothing in the building stopped them.

That metric forced some unusual choices.

We spent more on power than on furniture. Inverters with enough battery to bridge the switchover, so the generator starting is something you hear rather than something you feel. We spent more on the network than on the coffee machine — dual providers, automatic failover, a monitored uplink. We put the phone booths in before we put the branding up.

We also made the quiet zone genuinely quiet. Not "please keep it down" quiet. No-calls, no-meetings, headphones-optional quiet. It turns out a lot of people will pay for a room where they are allowed to concentrate.

## What we got wrong at first

Plenty.

We over-indexed on events early on. Three a week, decent turnout, and a members' survey that politely told us we were making the building harder to work in. We cut it to one a month and the satisfaction numbers went *up*. Community is not the same thing as programming.

We also priced only in monthly plans, which quietly excluded exactly the people we wanted — freelancers between contracts, students, founders who fly in for a week. Day and week rates went up on the site, in naira, no sales call required. That single change brought in a third of our current members.

## Where this goes

Forge is a workspace. Not an accelerator, not a school. We tried adjacent things and learned that we are better at running one room extremely well than at running four things adequately.

So the roadmap is unromantic: more power headroom, more phone booths, a second quiet floor, and better numbers published openly — uptime, average network latency, desk availability.

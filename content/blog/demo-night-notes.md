Demo night is the only recurring event we kept. Everything else we tried — talks, workshops, mixers — either fizzled out or made the building worse to work in.

Here is what a year of running it taught us, mostly by getting it wrong first.

## Five minutes, hard stop

We started with ten-minute slots. By the fourth demo the room had stopped listening.

Five minutes with an actual timer on screen fixed it. Nobody has ever complained that a demo was too short. Several people have thanked us for cutting one off.

The constraint also improves the demos themselves. Ten minutes invites a slide deck. Five minutes forces you to open the thing and show it working.

## Show, do not describe

The single rule we enforce: **the software has to run.**

No roadmaps, no mockups presented as products, no "imagine if". If it does not run, it is not ready for demo night — and that is fine, come back next month.

This sounds harsh and is actually the kindest rule we have. It means nobody spends a week making slides, and it means the audience gets a reliable signal: everything they saw tonight exists.

## Failure is allowed and should be

The best demo we have had was a build that crashed live. The presenter debugged it in front of forty people, narrating, and found the bug in about ninety seconds.

Nobody remembers the polished ones. Everybody remembers that.

We now say explicitly at the start that broken demos are welcome. It measurably increased the number of people willing to sign up — mostly the people who had been telling themselves their project was not ready.

## Feedback needs a shape

Open Q&A after each demo goes badly. It attracts the one person who wants to talk about their own project, and it intimidates first-timers.

What works: one question, from the host, chosen to be genuinely curious rather than probing. Everything else moves to the room afterwards. The presenter stands by the food and people come to them.

## The stuff that seems trivial

- **Start on time.** Once you slip twenty minutes, everyone learns to arrive twenty minutes late, permanently.
- **Food before, not after.** People eat, then settle. Food afterwards means half the room leaves during the last demo.
- **Sign-ups close at eight, not at capacity.** A fixed cutoff is fairer than a race and easier to plan.
- **Do not record by default.** Recording changes what people are willing to show. We ask each presenter, and most say no.

## What we would do differently

We should have kept a list from the beginning. Not attendance — projects. What was demoed, by whom, and what state it was in.

A year in, the most interesting thing about demo night is not any single evening. It is watching the same project appear three times across six months and get visibly, unmistakably better. We only have that history for the last five months because it did not occur to us to write it down.

If you are starting something like this: keep the list from night one.
